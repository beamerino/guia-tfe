# Using generative AI

Generative AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot…) can help you a lot or get you into serious trouble. The difference lies in **what** you use them for, **how you check** what they give you and **whether you declare it**.

!!! warning "Regulations first"
    Every university has (or is preparing) its own policy on AI in final projects. **Read it before you start** and, if in doubt, ask your supervisor. What follows is general good practice and doesn't replace your institution's rules.

## The underlying principle

**You are responsible for everything in your thesis**: every sentence, data point, reference and line of code. The AI doesn't sign your work or defend it before the committee. If you can't explain something, it shouldn't be there.

## Reasonable and problematic uses

| Generally acceptable (and declared) | Problematic |
|---|---|
| Having it explain a concept or method you don't understand, then checking against sources | Generating text and presenting it as your own |
| Brainstorming synonyms or terms for your search string | Asking it for references and citing them without checking |
| Checking spelling, grammar or clarity of text you wrote | Having it write chapters or the discussion for you |
| Helping you debug or document your code, understanding every change | Having it analyse your data or interpret your results without you verifying |
| Practising your defence: having it ask you committee-style questions | Uploading personal, clinical or confidential data |

## The three main risks

### 1. Made-up references

Language models **invent references** that look real: plausible authors, journals that exist, believable titles… and papers that were never published. They also attribute claims to real papers that those papers don't make. **Never cite anything you haven't found and read yourself.** Always search the [academic databases](estado-del-arte.md#where-to-search).

### 2. Errors that look true

Answers sound confident even when they're wrong: formulas misapplied, code that runs but does something else, invented figures. Check everything you use.

### 3. Data and confidentiality

What you type into many of these tools may be stored or used to train models. **Never upload** patient or participant data, company data, unpublished results or anything subject to confidentiality or the GDPR.

## How to declare it

If you use generative AI, **say so explicitly**, usually in a short section after the introduction, in the methodology or in an appendix, as your university specifies. Include:

- **Which tool**, and its version or date.
- **What** you used it for.
- **In which parts** of the project.
- **How you verified** the output.

> *Example:* "During the preparation of this project, [tool, version] was used to check the grammar and clarity of chapters 1 and 6, and to help debug the preprocessing code described in section 4.1. All suggestions were reviewed by the author, who takes full responsibility for the content."

## Think about your defence

At your defence you'll have to explain every decision in your own words. If an AI solved part of the work and you don't fully understand it, **it will show in the committee's questions**. Use AI to learn faster, not to skip the learning.
